\title{Putting the Count Back Into Accountability}
\subtitle{Analyzing Transparency Data About the Sexual Exploitation of Minors}
\credits

\begin{abstract}

Based on transparency disclosures for 17~US-based internet platforms and the
national clearinghouse collecting legally mandated reports about child sexual
exploitation (\V{CSE}), this study answers two research questions: What does the
data tell us about the growth of online \V{CSE}? How reliable is that data?
First, we analyze the growth in \V{CSE} reports over the last 25~years to derive
a model and identify the underlying population. Second, we perform two
comparative audits to assess the quality of transparency data. Critical findings
include: First, the rapid growth of reports over the last decade is linear and
largely driven by an equivalent growth in social media accounts. Second, the
Covid-19 pandemic had no statistically relevant impact on yearly report volume.
Third, while half of the surveyed organizations release meaningful transparency
data, the other half either fail to make disclosures or release data with severe
quality issues.

\end{abstract}

\keywords{social media, transparency reporting, audit, child sexual abuse
materials, National Center for Missing and Exploited Children, CyberTipline}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Public debate about child sexual exploitation (\V{CSE}) features many adamant to
alarming claims. There are the headlines in national newspapers announcing that
``the internet is overrun with images of child sexual
abuse''~\cite{KellerDance2019}, ``Instagram connects vast pedophile
network''~\cite{HorwitzBlunt2023}, and ``\V{AI} is about to make the online
child sex abuse problem much worse''~\cite{Oremus2024}. There are Senate
hearings on ``big tech and the online child sexual exploitation crisis''
featuring a who-is-who of social media \V{CEO}s~\cite{Durbin2024} and laws
referencing an ``explosion in the multijurisdictional distribution of child
pornography''~\cite{UnitedStatesCongress2008}. There also are the Moms for
Liberty, Qanon adherents, and Proud Boys mobilizing to ``\#SaveTheChildren''
while maligning everyone else, particularly \V{LGBT} folk, as ``pedos'' or
``groomers''~\cite{BuntainBarlowea2022, MartinyLawrence2023}. Even experts such
as academics~\cite{SalterWoodlockea2023}, lawyers~\cite{Haney2021b}, hotline
operators~\cite{ODonnell2021}, or police~\cite{Europol2020, Interpol2020} are
not above making lurid claims about the impact of the Covid-19 pandemic on
\V{CSE}.

Given the gravity of the topic and the creeping sensationalism infusing even the
law, two questions stand out:
\begin{enumerate}
\item What does available data tell us about the extent of online child sexual
exploitation?
\item How accurate and trustworthy is that data?
\end{enumerate}
Ready availability of accurate data can help ground public debate and experts'
contributions. It also is indispensable for designing or evaluating effective
interventions, allocating content moderation and law enforcement resources, and
predicting future trends and developments.

This article seeks to answer the two research questions based on transparency
data about CyberTipline reports released by the technology firms filing the vast
majority of them and the \V{US} not-for-profit receiving them, the National
Center for Missing and Exploited Children (\V{NCMEC}). According to the \V{US}
criminal code~\cite{USCode18Chapter110, USCode18Section1591}, so-called
electronic service providers (\V{ESP}s) must report all online \V{CSE} to
\V{NCMEC}, lest they face financial penalties and lose their safe harbor
protections against content liability. In turn, \V{NCMEC} triages reports,
forwarding them to suitable law enforcement agencies, both within the United
States and internationally. It also maintains databases with fingerprints of
pictures and videos that qualify as child sexual abuse material (\V{CSAM}) and
makes them available to service providers.

Each CyberTipline report describes an incident of child sexual exploitation,
with the statutory ``child'' being any person under the age of 18, i.e., a
\emph{minor}. Reportable activities include the production, distribution, and
possession of \V{CSAM}, sex trafficking of minors, sex tourism targeting minors,
sexual molestation of minors, the online enticement of minors including grooming
and sextortion, sending obscene material to minors, and misleading domain names
as well as hosted content. Beyond capturing the statutory category, the schema
for CyberTipline reports~\cite{NCMEC2024} includes fields that describe the
incident itself, e.g., date, time, platform, and involved persons, track the
involvement of law enforcement, enable attachment of original content, and
provide helpful context for triage, e.g., labels to identify memes, viral
content, possible self-production, live streaming, generative \V{AI}, and the
Tech Coalition classification~\cite{TechCoalition2022a}. The latter comprises
four labels to distinguish between (A)~pre-pubescent and (B)~post-pubescent as
well as (1)~sex act and (2)~lascivious exhibition. A few actual CyberTipline
reports (without attachments) have become public as part of court
proceedings~\cite{CyberTipline5074778, CyberTipline5778397}.

Three aspects of transparency disclosures about CyberTipline reports make them
particularly well-suited to answering the two research questions. First,
platforms' community guidelines differ
substantially~\cite{BatemanThompsonea2021,JiangMiddlerea2020}, are enforced
inconsistently~\cite{Common2020,DiazHechtFelella2021}, and are reflected in
transparency disclosures to varying degrees~\cite{UrmanMakhortykh2023}. As a
result, it is generally difficult, if not impossible, to aggregate or compare
service providers' transparency data. However, since they are produced through a
statutory mechanism, CyberTipline reports are an exception: Their counts
\emph{can} be aggregated over time and service providers; they also \emph{can}
be compared over time for the same provider or between (some) providers and
\V{NCMEC}.

Second, content curation and moderation are key features of contemporary,
centralized social media that engage in surveillance
capitalism~\cite{Zuboff2019} as a business model---if nothing else to keep the
platform's content sufficiently inoffensive so that advertisers won't
bail~\cite{Masnick2022a,Patel2022a}. Alas, that same curation and moderation
effort raises thorny questions about platform governance~\cite{Klonick2018},
with transparency reports serving as an important accountability
mechanism~\cite{AccessNowACLUFoundationOfNorthernCaliforniaea2021,
EuropeanParliamentAndCouncil2022}. Since social media also are the currently
dominant means for dissemination of \V{CSAM}~\cite{SteelNewmanea2020}, their
transparency reports are bound to capture much of that violative content as
well.

Third, the legal requirement to file CyberTipline reports only applies to
service providers operating out of the United States. At the same time, many
American internet platforms have global reach. For instance, at the end Of 2024,
Meta alone had 3.9 billion daily active people across all its platforms---or
41\% of the world population~\cite{MetaPlatformsInc2025}. Hence, their
transparency data captures violative content and behaviors from all across the
world. In fact, from 2019 t0 2024, only between 5.2\% and 15.8\% out of the 17.0
to 36.2~million (\V{M}) reports received by \V{NCMEC} every year were about
incidents in the United States; the vast majority happened outside the country.

At the same time, transparency data about CyberTipline reports is not a panacea
when characterizing online \V{CSE}. It fundamentally depends on organizations
noticing incidents of \V{CSE}, filing CyberTipline reports about them, and also
making the attendant transparency disclosures. That is the case for most of the
larger centralized platforms operated by \V{US} corporations. But it holds to a
lesser degree for smaller platforms and federated social media, which lack
resources and tools for comparable trust and safety efforts~\cite{RothLai2024}.
Finally, it does not hold for the dark web, which is designed for anonymity and
hence for avoiding accountability. Still, when using appropriate methods,
transparency data about CyberTipline reports can yield considerable insights.

\label{sec:methods}

In discussing methods (here) and findings (below), we adhere to the two research
questions for structuring the presentation. All data and Python


\subsection{The Deluge of CyberTipline Reports}

To characterize the extent of online \V{CSE}, we start by considering yearly
CyberTipline report counts from inception of the tipline in 1998 to 2024
(inclusive, like all year ranges in this article) and then narrow temporal and
geographical scopes for additional insight. In addition to utilizing familiar
descriptive statistics, our analysis compares the goodness-of-fit for linear
models, all but one using a single independent variable and report counts as
dependent variable, based on adjusted $R^2$. We first characterize overall
growth in CyberTipline reports by comparing exponential growth, i.e., a linear
model over the logarithm of the dependent variable, with piecewise-linear growth
for the full year range. Next, to identify the likely underlying population
driving that growth, we narrow the temporal scope to 2014--2024 and compare
linear models for human population, internet users, and social media accounts.
To add detail, we narrow both temporal and geographical scopes and compare
rankings of per-country report counts normalized by human population as well as
social media accounts from 2020 to 2024. Finally, to explore the impact of the
Covid-19 pandemic, we restore global scope and compare standard residuals for
the linear growth model, using the customary two standard residuals as threshold
for identifying outliers.

The primary source of data are \V{NCMEC}'s transparency disclosures to the
congressional appropriations committees, which include total report counts going
back to 1998 but otherwise cover only 2020--2024, as well its per-country
breakdowns, which cover 2019--2024. Our analysis also draws on datasets about
human population (United Nations), internet users (International
Telecommunication Union), and social media accounts (DataReportal).
Machine-readable files for all data and hyperlinks to their sources are included
with the supplemental materials for this article (\emph{citation elided for
review}); we discuss data curation in more detail below.


\subsection{Comparative Data Audits}

To characterize the quality of transparency data, we validate that service
providers' transparency disclosures include meaningful metrics for online
\V{CSE} and, if they do, assess to what degree repeated disclosures of the same
quantities diverge in two different ways. The first such audit checks the
\emph{internal consistency} of computer-readable data, i.e., \V{CSV} files,
released by the same service provider. For each reporting period~$p+1$, it
compares all entries shared with the previous period~$p$, which are the entries
for periods 1~to~$p$ making up the file for period~$p$. Since this audit
compares the same metrics published by the same organization some number of
months apart, we expect that values only change as a result of deliberate
modification, notably, to correct incomplete or buggy disclosures. Hence,
differences should exhibit temporal or logical locality, i.e., be systemic
errors. Descriptive statistics suffice for checking whether this is indeed the
case.

The second audit checks the \emph{external consistency} of CyberTipline report
counts as disclosed by service providers and \V{NCMEC} in its per-provider
breakdown. Since the latter provides only yearly counts and, in 2019--2020, did
so only for entire corporations, this audit also compares yearly per-corporation
counts. Since this audit compares the results of two independent organizations
tabulating the same unidirectional message flows, we expect some small degree of
divergence that is proportional to the number of messages and caused by
(non-deterministic) software bugs or hardware outages. Differences should not
exhibit discernible patterns, i.e., be random errors.

We characterize concordance with the intra-class correlation coefficient
(\V{ICC}) for count data~\cite{Carrasco2022}. However, due to the small number
of per-year, per-provider samples, with $1\leq{}n\leq{}6$ and
$\overline{n}=3.7$, we could only compute a goodness-of-fit for the entire
dataset, across all nine providers, with $N=33$. When comparing a Poisson model
and negative binomial models with variance growing linearly as well as
quadratically with mean, only the negative binomial with quadratic variance has
an acceptable goodness-of-fit.

Since we cannot validate the model for individual providers, we also consider
Bland-Altman or mean-difference plots~\cite{BlandAltman1995} for each provider
individually and also all of them together. Consistent with the assumption that
error for CyberTipline report counts grows with magnitude, we plot percentage
differences instead of absolute values. We use the mean as denominator, since it
provides an unbiased best guess for the true value in absence of other
information. Furthermore, since the normality assumption does not hold for count
data, we omit the customary 95\% limits of agreement and instead trace the mean
difference. We then use visual inspection to identify patterns and use the sign
test to confirm their statistical significance.

We select candidates for the two audits based on three criteria, applied to
service providers and their subsidiaries: Being a popular social media site
based on Buffer's yearly rankings from 2022--2024~\cite{Lua2022, Lua2023,

comparing CyberTipline report counts with \V{NCMEC}. Note that Omegle, a video

\subsection{Data Curation}

Perusal of even a few hyperlinks in that inventory makes clear that most of the
disclosures are \emph{not} machine-readable. To accurately capture the data in
machine-readable form, we manually transcribed and validated disclosures with a
small number of quantities. For larger tables, we relied on advanced editor
features and ad-hoc scripts to restore tabular data extracted from \V{HTML} and
\V{PDF} documents with copy \& paste, while also making sure that the quantities
themselves were preserved as is. For DataReportal's social media accounts data,
which is spread across approximately 1,200 slides formatted as \V{SVG} outlines,
two people performed the manual data entry across three sessions on separate
days. During a session, one person read each quantity out loud, and the other
person entered the datum and read it back. For Meta's \V{CSV} files, which are
not archived by the firm, we supplemented files downloaded from Meta's website
over the last three years with copies from the Internet Archive going back to
\V{Q2}~2021.

In curating disclosures, we generally preserve a provider's terminology and
numerical representation. We make three exceptions that are grounded in
CyberTipline reporting and hence have a well-defined meaning. They are
\emph{reports} for the number of CyberTipline reports that were filed by the
provider, \emph{pieces} for the number of pictures, videos, and other documents
that were attached to CyberTipline reports, and \emph{accounts} for the number
of user accounts that were terminated for their involvement.

To map service providers' metrics to the three standard categories, we make the
following accommodations. First, we treat the common euphemism ``permanent
suspension'' as account termination. Next, in lieu of a better match, we use
\V{CSAM} counts as pieces. While that may fail to capture some violative
content, 96.8\% of all reports received by \V{NCMEC} in 2024 involve \V{CSAM}
and at least 99.2\% of all reports for the four year before do the same.
Finally, for Meta, we treat its ``child nudity \& sexual exploitation'' category
as pieces. In \V{Q2}~2021, Meta switched to the more precise ``child
endangerment: sexual exploitation,'' which we also treat as pieces. Including
the earlier, imprecise category maximizes coverage for the service provider
filing at least 91.5\% of all reports in 2019--2021.

\subsection{The Deluge of CyberTipline Reports}
\label{sec:findings:reports}

Figure~\ref{fig:reports} shows three different views onto yearly CyberTipline
report counts, each with a different scale. First, the blue line in the upper
panel tracks the absolute number of reports in millions from 1998--2024, with
the right y-axis covering the range from 0 to 40\V{M} reports. Second, the
orange line in the upper panel tracks the number of reports per 1,000 social
media accounts, with the left y-axis covering the range from~0 to~8 reports per
1k accounts. For both curves, gray lines indicate the linear least squares fit
for the decade from~2014 to~2023. Despite the different scales, both curves
converge on the same point for 2024, hence the orange mark in addition to the
blue line. Third, the magenta bars in the lower panel track the standard
residuals for report counts during the same decade, with the right y-axis
covering the range from~-2 to~2 standard deviations. Each of the three views
illustrates one of the following findings.


\begin{finding}
Yearly changes in the number of CyberTipline reports are best modelled as
piecewise-linear with three distinct epochs, from~1998 to~2013, from~2014
to~2023, and for 2024.
\end{finding}

\noident{}A 2019 study by authors affiliated with Google, \V{NCMEC}, and Thorn
characterized the growth of CyberTipline reports as
exponential~\cite{BurszteinBrightea2019}, and \V{NCMEC} has been doing the same
in its transparency disclosures to the congressional appropriations committees
for 2022, 2023, and 2024~\cite{NcmecOjjdp2022, NcmecOjjdp2023, NcmecOjjdp2024}.
The assertion seems unwarranted after a 43.4\% drop in report counts for 2024.
Furthermore, even when excluding report counts for 2024, the fit of a
piecewise-linear model with discontinuity in 2014 (adjusted $R^2 = 0.9911$) is
better than that of an exponential model (adjusted $R^2 = 0.954$). Also, the
mean squared error of the piecewise-linear model is $15\times$ smaller than that
of the exponential model.

\V{NCMEC} attributes much of the drop in 2024 to a change in reporting
practices. Starting that year, service providers can file a single report for
viral content and memes that qualify as \V{CSAM} instead of filing a report each
time that same content is posted. That way, the 20.5\V{M} filed reports account
for 29.2\V{M} incidents~\cite{NcmecCyberTipline2024}. \V{NCMEC} notes that the
decline by 7\V{M} from the previous year's peak of 36.2\V{M} reports is
unexpected, especially since child sex trafficking and online enticement became
reportable that same year. It then blames Meta's deployment of end-to-end
encryption (\V{E2EE}) for that difference. Alas, that underestimates the
magnitude of the difference because if the growth of the prior decade had
continued, 3.93~million more reports than in 2023 would have been filed in 2024,
making for a difference of 10.9\V{M} reports.


\begin{finding}
The likely population driving the absolute yearly increase of 3.93 million
reports over the decade from 2014 to 2023 is social media accounts. That does
not, however, account for a relative increase from roughly one to almost eight
reports per 1,000 social media user accounts over the same period.
\end{finding}

\noindent{}When considering only 2014--2023, a linear model relating report
counts to social media accounts as independent variable (adjusted $R^2 = 0.980$)
has a better fit than that for years (adjusted $R^2 = 0.977$), world population
(adjusted $R^2 = 0.966$), internet users (adjusted $R^2 = 0.964$) and years as
well as social media accounts (adjusted $R^2 = 0.978$). Being reported for
online \V{CSE} requires internet access, hence the model for social media
accounts having a better fit than that for human population seems reasonable.
Since the social media amongst surveyed platforms account for 92.6\%--96.2\% of
all CyberTipline reports from 2019--2024, the model for social media accounts
having a better fit than that for internet users also seems reasonable. However,
we wouldn't necessarily expect the model for human population to have a better
than that for internet users, even if the difference in adjusted $R^2$ is rather
small. That raises the question of whether the differences in adjusted $R^2$ are
meaningful.

To explore that question, we increase the resolution to per-country report
counts, narrow the scope to~2020--2024, and compare reports per capita with
reports per social media accounts. When ranking countries by the former,
$9\leq{}n\leq{}14$, $\overline{n}=12.2$ of the worst thirty countries are
members of the Arab League, a phenomenon that has no obvious explanation and was
prominently noted by~\citet{Childlight2024} as well. The fact that the country
with the most reports per capita alternates between Libya and the United Arab
Emirates (\V{UAE}) makes this even more puzzling. After all, the two countries
are substantially different---with Libya spending much of the 2010s in a state
of civil war~\cite{GlobalConflictTracker2024} and the \V{UAE} being more stable
and denser populated, while also having better infrastructure and larger
\V{GDP}.

social media accounts in 2023. First-ranked Yemen, with 79.1~r/ksoc, stands out
against the rest of the world due to its red-black shade. Thanks to their
saturated red shades, Afghanistan with 55.3~r/ksoc and Bangladesh with
47.8~r/ksoc also are clearly noticeable. All other countries have comparably
smaller rates, at most 37.6~r/ksoc, and hence are shaded orange or yellow.
}

In addition to being more clearly differentiated from lower ranked countries,
the countries with the highest rates per social media accounts more consistently
are troublespots. In~2020 and~2021, the country with the highest reports per
social media accounts is Eritrea. While Afghanistan and Yemen are ranked second-
and third-worst in 2021, they are the worst ranked two countries for 2022--2024.
Common to all three countries is that they were recently engaged in (civil)
wars~\cite{GlobalConflictTracker2025, GlobalConflictTracker2025a,
GlobalConflictTracker2025b}, with the attendant lawlessness apparently also
making the online sexual exploitation of minors worse.

While worst-rated countries have rates in the double digits, rates for the vast
majority of countries for both human population and social media accounts are
consistently and noticeably smaller. Notably, at least 86.5\% out of the 237
ranked countries have a rate of at most 4 r/kcap from 2020 to 2024. Likewise, at
least 85.6\% have a rate of at most 8 r/ksoc, which also equals the global rate
for~2023.

Overall, social media filing the vast majority of CyberTipline reports weighs
strongly in favor of treating social media accounts as the underlying
population. Its per country ranking having a larger domain and more clearly
differentiating countries with the highest rates is helpful when country
rankings are required. That global and per country rates are closely aligned is
reassuring. That the worst-ranked countries consistently are global
troublespots, however, cuts both ways: The consistency can be seen as reassuring
or as a flag for inaccurate data, since conducting representative population
surveys in warzones would seem unusually fraught with threats to validity.

While social media accounts seems like a strong contender for population driving
much of the growth in CyberTipline report counts, it definitely is not the only
factor. After all, the normalized reports per social media accounts are not
nearly constant and grow linearly from approximately 1~r/ksoc in 2014 to
8~r/ksoc in 2023. The drop down to 4~r/ksoc in 2024 suggests that a substantial
fraction of reports over the decade before that year is the result of memes and
other unwelcome content that is not intended to sexually exploit children. While
we lack the data for identifying other factors with confidence, we will note
that the number of service providers filing reports has grown from 230 in~2021
to 294 in~2024. Moreover, service providers, including Meta~\cite{Meta2023a},
Reddit~\cite{Reddit2025}, and Snap~\cite{Snap2024}, acknowledged improved
detection of \V{CSAM} and more generally \V{CSE} in recent transparency
disclosures, which points towards improved detection as a major remaining
contributor to the increase.


\begin{finding}
The Covid-19 pandemic had no statistically relevant impact on the yearly volume
of CyberTipline reports.
\end{finding}

\noindent{}As illustrated by the magenta bars in Figure~\ref{fig:reports}, the
standard residuals for CyberTipline reports are not particularly large, ranging
from~-1.7 to~1.5 standard deviations. In other words, the data is noisy when
compared to its model, but there are no pronounced outliers. Furthermore,
with~-1.2 and~+1.0, the standard residuals for the pandemic years 2020 and 2021
are clearly smaller, i.e., well within the noise. Finally, when considering
year-over-year growth, the increase of +0.5 standard deviations from 2019 to
2020 actually was on the smaller end. In other words, the growth in CyberTipline
report volume for the pandemic years is consistent with the growth for the rest
of the decade.

Even though no residual is larger than the customary two-standard-residuals
threshold for outliers, the report count did increase by 2.221 standard
residuals from~2020 to~2021. That is large enough to possibly include a
substantial pandemic-induced increase: Under this scenario, content moderators
had reduced reviewing capacity for much of 2020 and allowed a moderation backlog
to build up. Towards the end of 2020, they finally found their pandemic routine,
nimbly recovered from their slump, and started drawing down the backlog. As a
direct result, they filed a good number of additional CyberTipline reports in
2021. Our analysis of the European Commission's Digital Services Act
Transparency Database shows that a delay of roughly 100 days from content being
posted to being actioned is average for protection of minors policies. Under the
above scenario that delay would grow about threefold, which does not seem
unreasonable. Still, the number of additional CyberTipline reports that might
have resulted from such a scenario is capped by the 2.221 standard residual
difference between~2020 and~2021. As such, it statistically is within the noise.

We also reviewed previous work about the impact of the Covid-19 pandemic on the
online sexual exploitation of minors. Besides a blog post from April 2021 by
\V{NCMEC} that features only year-over-year comparisons~\cite{ODonnell2021} and
a university's research report from May 2021 that nicely captures the pressures
faced by individual hotline staff and police officers during the
pandemic~\cite{SalterWong2021}, peer-reviewed articles~\cite{Dabrowska2021,
Haney2021b, SalterWoodlockea2023} consistently cite reports by Europol from June
2020~\cite{Europol2020} and Interpol from September 2020~\cite{Interpol2020}.

Both reports feature mostly anecdotal and indirect evidence. Both reports also
misrepresent data about peer-to-peer networks by the Child Rescue Coalition.
They claim that graphs of sinusoidal signals that happen to rise between
February and April 2020 somehow demonstrate a ``significant increase in the
sharing of \V{CSEAM}'' (child sexual exploitation and abuse material)---to use
Interpol's boldface phrase. In addition to sharing the same sourcing and
description, Interpol's single curve very much looks like the sum of Europol's
four country-specific curves. That suggests that both police agencies are
presenting the same data. However, according to y-axis labels, Interpol's counts
are roughly $5,700\times$ larger than Europol's aggregates, suggesting that the
misrepresentation isn't the only problem with that data.

Having said that, Europol's report does contain compelling evidence that the
pandemic did, in fact, result in noticeably more incidents involving \V{CSAM},
albeit \emph{at a different timescale}. When looking at CyberTipline reports
\V{NCMEC} forwarded to European law enforcement agencies during the first half
of 2020 at \emph{monthly} granularity, there is a $5\times$ spike for March 2020
only. Given the date, the pandemic is the most plausible cause. Alas, the effect
is not sustained enough to be detectable in yearly statistics.


\begin{finding}
The publicly available transparency data neither proves nor disproves that
end-to-end encryption is a major cause for the steep decline of CyberTipline
reports in 2024. But it does suggest a plausible alternative explanation.
\end{finding}

\noindent{}In late 2023, just as Meta was starting to update its Messenger chat
service to utilize \V{E2EE} by default, \V{NCMEC} published a blog post
describing Meta's decision to do so a ``devastating blow to child
protection''~\cite{NCMEC2023}. Then, in its transparency disclosures for 2024,
the organization bemoans the negative impact of \V{E2EE} on Meta's report
volume~\cite{NcmecCyberTipline2024, NcmecOjjdp2024}. The drop in Meta's
reports---from 30.7\V{M} reports in 2023 to 13.8\V{M} reports in
2024---certainly is steep. \V{E2EE} may very well have been a contributing
factor. But the publicly available data points towards a more complex reality
than \V{NCMEC} acknowledges.

To begin with, Meta's WhatsApp service has been using end-to-end encryption
since 2016; with 2~billion users compared to Messenger's 1~billion, it also is
the more popular chat service. Yet despite WhatsApp's encryption, Meta has been
moderating messages and reporting those involving \V{CSE} to
\V{NCMEC}~\cite{ElkindGillumea2021}. First, metadata is not encrypted and may
suffice for detecting some suspicious activities, for example, when an adult
keeps interacting with minors even though there are no familial or professional
reasons for doing so. Second, when reporting another user, the WhatsApp client,
which does have access to the unencrypted message content, forwards up to five
messages sent by the user being reported to Meta's content moderators. Messenger
incorporates the same capability. The only difference is that Meta does not
disclose the number of forwarded messages; for unencrypted chats, it is thirty.

\includegraphics[scale=0.7]{figure-meta}

(right). The dashed lines indicates major changes to Meta's metrics, with the

\label{fig:meta}

\V{NCMEC}'s own transparency disclosures demonstrate that these monitoring
mechanisms have impact in practice. The hotline started distinguishing between
Meta's brands in its transparency disclosures in 2021. Over the four years to
2024, WhatsApp submitted 1.4, 1.0, 1.4, and 1.9 million CyberTipline reports.
These figures are substantial: Besides Meta, only Google, Snap, and TikTok filed
more than a million reports in a year. They also represent a year-over-year
increase of 33.2\% for 2024. That implies that Facebook and Instagram are
responsible for Meta's overall decline that year. Even if we are inclined to
believe that the latter was mostly caused by \V{E2EE}, WhatsApp's increase over
the same time period happened \emph{despite} \V{E2EE}. Furthermore, Snap
deployed \V{E2EE} for photos and videos in 2019, yet has been filing more
reports year-over-year for every year since~\cite{TeunissenNapier2022}.

Meta's disclosures of piece counts provide further evidence that \V{E2EE} may
not be the primary cause of the overall decline in reports. As illustrated in
the right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:meta}, Meta's overall piece count
substantially declined in both 2023 as well as 2024. Meta started deploying
\V{E2EE} in December 2023. So even if we exclude \V{Q4}~2023, the left panel of
Figure~\ref{fig:meta} shows that pieces substantially declined year-over-year
for the first three quarters, too---when Meta had not even begun deploying
\V{E2EE}. The fact that reports increased while pieces decreased in 2023 implies
a shift from image- and video-based \V{CSE}, notably \V{CSAM}, to other forms of
\V{CSE}, notably online enticement. While \V{E2EE} does make detection of
\V{CSAM} harder, metadata can help identify the latter by tracing inappropriate
contact between adults and minors. In summary, none of this disproves the
potential impact of \V{E2EE}. But it also doesn't support \V{E2EE} having this
impact and suggests some independent factor as plausible alternative.

\subsection{Comparative Data Audits}
\label{sec:findings:audits}

\subsubsection{Meaningful Metrics}

\begin{finding}
Discord's and TikTok's transparency disclosures suffer from poorly chosen
metrics, which render them largely useless for tracking online \V{CSE}. They
also use misleading language while introducing their respective metrics.
\end{finding}

\noindent{}Discord does not disclose the number of CyberTipline reports filed
but the number of accounts reported to \V{NCMEC}. That quantity is 34\%--83\%
smaller than the number of reports. While that is an interesting statistic, its
disclosure without additional context is problematic. First, since fewer
reported accounts than reports implies that the same accounts were reported
repeatedly, one wonders how they were able to do so more than once given the
firm's oft emphasized ``zero tolerance'' fir \V{CSE}. Likewise, one wonders why
the metric was picked. Its introduction in the transparency disclosure for
\V{H2}~2020 only reinforces that concern. The quantity was introduced in a
section titled ``Reports to \V{NCMEC}'' with a bar chart titled ``\V{H2}~2020
\V{NCMEC} Reports.'' Only the paragraph below the chart clarified that Discord
reported ``6,948 \emph{accounts} to \V{NCMEC}'' (emphasis ours).

TikTok discloses absolute counts for the number of actioned videos only; all
other metrics such as the breakdown into categories and subcategories of
violative content are expressed as decimal fractions. Before introduction of a
new schema in \V{Q2}~2023, the firm did not disclose all subcategory fractions,
omitting the fraction of videos actioned by automation, and thereby made it
impossible to calculate the number of videos actioned because of \V{CSAM}. Upon
introduction of the new schema, TikTok claimed that it ``added granularity.''
While it did increase the number of subcategories from 26 to 29, it also removed
subcategories, notably reducing minor safety subcategories from five to four.
That change means that it remains impossible to calculate the number of videos
actioned because of \V{CSAM}. Meanwhile, TikTok's \V{CSV} files diverge in
capitalization for labels and serialization of floating point numbers, e.g., the
same quantity may appear as ``0.9620000000000001'' and ``0.962.''


\subsubsection{Internal Consistency}

\begin{finding}
In addition to being the only service provider to disclose only rounded
quantities, an average of 3.6\% of Meta's transparency disclosures from
\V{Q3}~2021 to \V{Q1}~2023 diverges quarter-over-quarter. Divergent entries vary
widely in metrics and quarters, with no apparent temporal or logical locality.
\end{finding}

\noindent{}For seven quarters, from \V{Q3}~2021 to \V{Q1}~2023, Meta's \V{CSV}
file for that quarter diverges from the previous quarter's \V{CSV} file for
$0.6\%\leq{}n\leq{}7.3\%$, $\overline{n}=3.6\%$ of all entries. Divergent
entries vary widely in policy area and actual metric (e.g., content actioned,
appealed, or restored). Notably, 12, 3, 7, 7, 11, 17, and 4 entries about
\V{CSE} differ from the previous quarter over the seven quarters, respectively.
Divergent entries also vary widely in the affected period, going back up to
7--11 quarters every quarter but \V{Q4}~2021 and \V{Q1}~2023. The latter two
quarters also are the quarters with the fewest divergent entries, i.e., 1.2\%
and 0.6\%, respectively. Overall, there is no discernible temporal or logical
locality to these errors. Meta never acknowledged these discrepancies. Though
they did stop just as the \V{EU}'s Digital Services Act was taking effect.

Alas, the actual extent of divergent entries across Meta's transparency data is
unknowable. Since Meta is the only service provider to round its transparency
data, additional disparities may very well have been erased by the rounding
process. By the same argument, we cannot exclude that Meta's data continues to
suffer from quality issues. They may just be small enough not to survive
rounding. The presence of disparities also raises the question of why Meta
decided to round transparency statistics in the first place.

Figure~\ref{fig:reports-audit} shows the mean difference plots for the nine

for each provider across nine panels and then for all providers in the tenth
panel. For each pair of report counts disclosed by a service provider and

\begin{finding}
Aylo's Pornhub is the only service provider to acknowledge and explain a mistake
in their handling of CyberTipline reports.
\end{finding}

\noindent{}When considering y-axis scales, the plot for one provider sticks out:
Aylo's 104.1\% difference for 2020. The firm only makes transparency disclosures
for its most visible brand, Pornhub. In its transparency report for the year,
which was published in April 2021, the brand not only acknowledges the
discrepancy but also explains it as the result of submitting the same reports
``multiple times in an abundance of caution.'' They add that \V{NCMEC} has been
notified of the duplicate reports. Strikingly, this is the only instance we
could find of a service provider acknowledging a problem with their disclosures
or providing a credible explanation for the problem.


\begin{finding}
For the nine service providers that disclose at least a year of CyberTipline
report counts, all but 4~out of~33 counts are within 5\% of \V{NCMEC}'s
disclosures. They also exhibit a very small but statistically relevant bias
towards underreporting.
\end{finding}

\noindent{}In addition to Aylo's outlier, there are three considerably smaller
ones at 16.2\% for Amazon, 17.1\% for Meta, and -16.1\% for Pinterest. We have
no additional insight into Amazon's and Pinterest's outliers. But we do have an
informed guess for Meta's outlier: The firm does not usually include WhatsApp in
its transparency disclosures. Sure enough, if we add WhatsApp's report count for
2024 as disclosed by \V{NCMEC} to the report count disclosed by Meta, the
percent difference ceases to be an outlier, coming down to 2.3\%. That would
also bring it into range with the remaining 29 percent differences, all of which
have magnitude smaller than 5\%. That's small enough to conclude that the other
eight service providers---Amazon, Aylo, Google, Microsoft, Pinterest, Reddit,
and Snap---make a good faith effort at data collection and disclosure. Then
again, even if we include the outliers, the intra-class correlation coefficient
is 0.9999, indicating very strong concordance.

Meanwhile, the mean difference plots make another pattern clearly discernible,
namely that the majority of percentage differences is positive. That holds for
individual values, with 20 positive, 7 zero, and 6 negative percentage
differences. That also holds for the means of the percentage differences, which
are indicated by a dashed line in each panel and positive for eight out of nine
providers as well as for all providers together. Even when disregarding the four
outliers, the remaining 29 percent differences still have a positive mean of
1.1\%. The sign test further validates that this bias is statistically
significant with a p-value of 0.0047.

In theory, positive percentage differences are either the result of providers
undercounting or \V{NCMEC} overcounting or both. In practice, it seems unlikely
that \V{NCMEC} would overcount for all providers but Reddit, which has the same
yearly report counts as \V{NCMEC} across five out of six years and five out of
seven equal pairs. In either case, with 1.1\% on average ignoring the four
outliers, the respective bias is very small.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

In trying to make approximate sense of the above findings, we start by noting
that \V{NCMEC}'s very mission is the protection of minors, whereas content
moderation is a means to an end for service providers, i.e., to ensure that
their platforms remain attractive to advertisers. Anything beyond that,
including CyberTipline reporting or transparency disclosures, probably requires
external incentives to justify the spend. We consider three scenarios for
service providers, each with its own incentives, and then turn to \V{NCMEC}. In
doing so, we identify larger take-away lessons.

The first scenario is based on the observation that \V{US} law does not mandate
affirmative scanning for \V{CSAM} and other forms of \V{CSE}. However, once a
service provider ``obtains actual knowledge of any facts or circumstances''
involving \V{CSE}, they must file a CyberTipline report ``as soon as reasonably
possible.''~(\S\V{2258A} of~\citet{USCode18Chapter110}). The same statutory
section also imposes strict record keeping and confidentiality requirements on
service providers. Give the general abhorrence of \V{CSE}, the administrative
overhead of CyberTipline reporting may incentivize some providers to try
ignoring \V{CSE} on their platforms. The problem with such a laissez-faire
approach to content moderation is that, in this day and age, it seems pretty
much impossible to maintain plausible deniability about \V{CSAM} and \V{CSE}.
That way, laissez-faire turns into willful negligence~\cite{SalterSokolov2024},
which can pierce safe harbor protections in case of sex trafficking. For
instance, the lawsuit that caused Omegle to shut down alleges negligence in half
of its claims~\cite{LongGoldbergea2022}.


\subsection{Predicting the Future}

The second scenario is based on the very real threat of losing safe harbor
protections in addition to financial penalties and a shattered reputation. When
service providers recognize that the combined threat is existential, content
moderation and CyberTipline reporting become the obviously preferable options.
Furthermore, once these controls are in place, the marginal cost for service
providers filing an extra CyberTipline report approaches zero, especially with
increased automation. That cost differential becomes a powerful incentive to
report any and all content and actions that could remotely be construed as
\V{CSE}, including memes or content shared in
outrage~\cite{GrossmanPfefferkornea2024}. In other words, service providers are
strongly incentivized to overreport. That, in turn, poses a major challenge for
\V{NCMEC}, which needs to process the combined report streams of all service
providers.

\begin{takeout}
With almost all reports encompassing \V{CSAM}, it is too coarse a criterion for
categorizing the content of CyberTipline reports. More granular accounting is
urgently needed.
\end{takeout}

\noindent{}Now, \V{NCMEC} enjoyed a reprieve from the crushing absolute number
of CyberTipline reports in 2024, down 43.5\% from the peak of 36.2\V{M} reports
the previous year. The question is how long will that reprieve last? We answer
the question based on two different ways of relating the old and new reporting
regimes. The first way is the obvious one, i.e., it is based on the ratio
between the compressed number of reports, i.e., 20.5\V{M} and the uncompressed
number of incidents, i.e., 29.2\V{M}. That gives us a scaling factor $\sigma_1
= 20.5 / 29.2 = 0.702$. Alas, we already know that this scaling factor is on the
strict side, as it accounts for memes and similar content only.

The second way utilizes \V{NCMEC}'s coarse ranking of reports as either
low-quality \emph{informational} or high-quality \emph{actionable} content,
which happens to be embedded in a table that also categorizes reports by type of
law enforcement recipient, for example, see~\citet{NcmecOjjdp2024}. We can
leverage this ranking because reports that capture more than one incident also
are informational. According to \V{NCMEC}'s disclosures, the percentage
fractions of actionable reports in 2020--2024 are 46.1\%, 62.6\%, 49.1\%,
50.3\%, and 78.4\%, respectively. Just as expected, the percentage fraction of
actionable reports increases substantially in 2024. More interestingly, in the
years 2020, 2022, and 2023, the percentage fractions of actionable reports are
close, with $\overline{n} = 48.5\%$. We ignore the outlier in 2021 and treat the
mean as the real percentage fraction for the old reporting regime. To derive the
second scaling-factor, we translate the fraction of informational reports for
the new regime to the equivalent fraction for the old regime (the informational
component) and add it to the fraction for actionable reports for the same regime
(the actionable component). That yields $\sigma_2 = 48.5\% + 48.5\% / 78.4\% *
21.6\% = 0.618$.

With the two scaling factors in place, we can now adjust the yearly report rate
$r = 3.93$\V{M} from our model for 2014--2023 to project growth from 2025
onward to be between $\sigma_2 r = 2.43$\V{M} and $\sigma_1 r = 2.76$\V{M}
reports per year. If we assume that 40\V{M} reports is \V{NCMEC}'s breaking
point, that gives the organization between 7 and 8~years until CyberTipline
reports come close to overwhelming its capacity again. This does assume that
social media accounts keep growing and \V{CSE} detection keeps improving at
current rates. If either slows down, then we need to adjust the model and
projections as well.

\begin{takeout}
As illustrated by these projections, meaningful analysis requires incorporating
events such as decline over 40\% of CyberTipline reports for~2024. But doing so
requires additional data, such as the relationship between reports and
incidents, and not just for the one abnormal year.
\end{takeout}


\subsection{Obstructing the Past}

The third scenario considers what to do \emph{after} all \V{CSE} has been
detected and all CyberTipline reports have been filed. Large service providers
with a substantial presence in the European Union are subject to that country
block's Digital Services Act (\V{DSA})~\cite{EuropeanParliamentAndCouncil2022},
which mandates transparency disclosures of aggregate statistics on an at least
yearly basis and of individual decisions, suitably stripped of personally
identifying information, on a daily basis.

We might expect that this substantial, but localized legal requirement would
encourage \V{US} service providers to rationalize their operation by instituting
the same transparency practices globally. So far, however, the Brussels effect
does not seem to have taken hold for transparency
disclosures~\cite{Bradford2020}. Besides the fact that the \V{DSA} only took
effect in 2023, two factors seriously complicate the release of transparency
disclosures. First, the audience for transparency data is generally ill-defined.
That means that service providers have little guidance on what metrics to
disclose. The Santa Clara
Principles~\cite{AccessNowACLUFoundationOfNorthernCaliforniaea2021} and \V{DSA}
provide some guidance but are surprisingly high-level and imprecise.

Second, while the audience for transparency disclosures remains relatively
ill-defined, the negative reputational impact of transparency disclosures can be
stark. We already highlighted some of the more sensationalist claims about
\V{CSE} in the introduction to this article. They result, in part, from
statistically unencumbered cherry-picking of transparency data, notably,
mistaking absolute CyberTipline report counts as a measure of prevalence, and an
information ecosystem that favors emotionally charged, viral, and conspiratorial
content. The combination thereof serves as a strong incentive for service
providers to withhold data or cherry-pick metrics themselves. While there is
some measure of grim satisfaction in watching social media being hoisted by
their own petard, the stakes for \V{CSE} are simply too high and the
consequences too grave to leave it at that.

In particular, the ill-defined requirements for transparency disclosures also
complicate validation thereof. Meta provides a telling example: The firm
commissioned two external audits. First, a panel of academic experts performed a
qualitative review of Meta's policies, enforcement process, and transparency
reporting and made several on-point recommendations for their
improvement~\cite{BradfordGriselea2019}. Second, Ernst \& Young performed a
quantitative assessment of Meta's transparency data collection~\cite{Meta2022}.
According to Meta, they went through ``in-depth preparation'' themselves and
provided \V{E\&Y} with full access to data as well as data scientists, software
engineers, and internal auditors. Yet both Meta and \V{E\&Y} didn't think to
compare metrics with those from the previous quarter and thereby missed
$27/2,192=1.2\%$ divergent entries for \V{Q4}~2021.

Meta also nicely illustrates the outcomes from this tension between transparency
and self-protection. The firm probably has invested more than any other service
provider into trust and safety and makes a number of regular transparency
disclosures. But it also has quite the histor of releasing faulty or misleading
metrics and favoring profits over minor safety. To list some highlights, Meta
repeatedly misreported advertising metrics to customers~\cite{BruellPatel2020,
VranicaMarshall2016}, platform usage data to a researchers~\cite{Timberg2021},
and adverts in its transparency database~\cite{Rosenberg2019}. When the latter
resulted in a string of critical press reports, Meta deplatformed the academics
who had collected the data~\cite{EdelsonMcCoy2021a}. It also shut down its
CrowdTangle transparency tool when it became inconvenient~\cite{Kerr2024}. As
far as minor safety is concerned, the firm ran an ad campaign sexualizing
teenage girls and targeting middle-aged straight men (for TikTok
nonetheless)~\cite{SilvermanMac2020}, tried to expand Instagram's teenage
audience despite knowing that the platform is toxic to teenage
girls~\cite{WellsHorwitzea2021}, and generally was reluctant to even consider
minor safety~\cite{SungSilberling2024}.

Meanwhile, this also study illustrates the potential of transparency data. It
critically relies on CyberTipline reporting imposing a uniform process on
participants and on \V{NCMEC} having markedly different incentives. Perhaps
reassuringly, the findings demonstrate that a sizable number of service
providers, i.e., half of surveyed organizations, act in good faith when
disclosing transparency data.

\begin{takeout}
Suitably designed regulation is uniquely suitable for establishing a shared
vocabulary for transparency disclosures and hence enables broader analysis. When
combined with redundant disclosures, ideally involving several independent
parties, it also facilitates validation of disclosures.
\end{takeout}

\noindent{}The usual discourse about service providers and \V{CSE} seems to be
based on an assumption that, given enough content moderation, online \V{CSE} can
be minimized if not eradicated. This article is certainly based on the
assumption that, given enough data on the spread of \V{CSE}, we can understand
the spread---and then develop more effective strategies for containing it. This
narrative has certainly been embraced by social media platforms. They often
treat transparency disclosures as opportunities for touting the effectiveness of
their stewardship of our online interactions.

The findings, however, suggest that the relationship between social media and
\V{CSE} is more complicated, we dare say, mutually reinforcing to some degree.
If we accept that \V{US}-based, corporate social media are designed for intense
emotional engagement and viral content, why would we expect that they have a
different impact on people with a pathological interest in children? If we
accept that social media have made it easier to show pictures and videos to a
potentially global audience than any other technology, why would be expect that
they have a different impact on \V{CSAM}? The rational answer is, of course,
that we shouldn't expect much of a difference.

Consider the growth in CyberTipline reports. Our analysis suggests that social
media accounts are the likely underlying population driving that growth from
2014 onwards. But it doesn't explain why this period of hypergrowth started in
that paricular year. Alas, observers of internet culture do: They identify just
that year as the year social media consolidated their hold on us~\cite{Cao2024,
Staltz2017}. A similarly strange phenomenon is that Arab League countries
consistently have high rates of reports per capita and social media accounts.
Given the differences between member countries, it stands to reason that some
cultural factor is behind these elevated rates. We leave identification of this
factor for future work, but will note that it may very well be extrinsic, e.g.,
caused by prejudice of content moderators.

\begin{takeout}
Reigning in the spread of online \V{CSE} without structural changes to social
media may just be impossible.
\end{takeout}


\subsection{Exaggerating the Present}

Besides operating the CyberTipline, \V{NCMEC} also helps locate missing children
(its original mission), maintains databases of \V{CSAM} hashes as well as
victims, and offers support services to victims, their families, and law
enforcement. It was chartered by law, is largely funded by the \V{US}
government, and works closely with law enforcement. For these reasons, courts
have found \V{NCMEC} to be a ``government agent'' that must abide by Fourth
Amendment protections against unreasonable
search~\cite{GrossmanPfefferkornea2024}, which complicates operation of the
CyberTipline. At the same time, \V{NCMEC} is not a law enforcement agency.
Instead it probably is best understood as an organization that seeks to protect
a uniquely vulnerable population, children, from some of the worst crimes and,
failing that, to support the victims and their families in their recovery.

That goes a long way towards explaining why \V{NCMEC} uses too broad a brush at
times when describing the impact of \V{CSE}. Examples from this study include
the insistence on characterizing the growth in CyberTipline reports as
exponential even after a substantial decline, or the reductive declaration that
Meta's drop in reported incidents must be caused by \V{E2EE}. At the same time,
the only reason that this study could complicate the picture for the examples
are \V{NCMEC}'s transparency disclosures. In fact, since 2022, when we started
this study, \V{NCMEC} has been improving its transparency disclosures every year
by enlarging the scope with additional metrics and by adding granularity to
existing statistics. That is an excellent track record, especially when compared
to service providers, and we sure hope \V{NCMEC} continues doing so.

\begin{takeout}
Given its globally significant role, \V{NCMEC}'s transparency disclosures are
uniquely important for understanding the spread of online \V{CSE}. Their
continued release and improvement are a major contribution.
\end{takeout}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In summary, the evidentiary record for CyberTipline reports does \emph{not}
support the more sensationalist claims about online child sexual exploitation.
There is \emph{no} ``explosion'' of \V{CSAM}. The internet is \emph{not}
``overrun with images of child sexual abuse.'' When taking yearly growth into
account, the pandemic did \emph{not} trigger more sexual exploitation overall,
even if there was a marked spike in March 2020.

Nonetheless, the evidentiary record does support the claim that \V{US}-based
social media, with their unprecedented global reach and propensity for virality,
are the biggest driving force behind the growth of CyberTipline reports over the
decade from 2014 to 2023. While that growth threatened to overwhelm \V{NCMEC},
i.e., the very organization serving as a critical global resource, improvements
to the reporting of repeated incidents combined with an unexpected decline in
reports for 2024 have resulted in a reprieve. Our projections based on a linear
model for growth and two different metrics for the decline in 2024 suggest that
\V{NCMEC} will be at breaking point in 7 to 8~years.

As far as transparency is concerned, many technology firms did better than we
expected. As this study has amply demonstrated, transparency disclosures needn't
be mere transparency theater, but can help produce real insight. The most
surprising result from this study is the finding that firms generally disclose
reasonably accurate CyberTipline report counts but nonetheless have a very small
but statistically significant bias towards underreporting. It would appear that
the professionalism of the trust and safety personnel preparing these statistics
prevails, but cannot entirely overcome all too human biases. That seems like a
very human result on a topic where most counts represent devastating human
implications.
